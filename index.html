Why

I built this for myself.

I kept saving videos I was never going to watch again. Talks, interviews, long podcasts, things I'd need one specific line out of six months later. They piled up in tabs and playlists and I never found anything twice.

So I made something that took a link and gave me back the video, everything said in it, and a short version I could skim. Then I kept adding the parts I wanted next. A place to keep it all. A way to search it. A way to pull the slides out of a talk. Eventually it was a real app, so I spent a while making it look like one.

That's the whole story. It does what I needed it to do, it's free, and it's yours if it's useful to you too.

— Dawid

Slides

Turn a talk into notes.

Point Sift at a recorded talk and it finds the slides — the real pictures of them, charts and text and all. It throws away the duplicates, so a slide left up for two minutes gives you one picture. Then it hands you a document: everything that was said, with each slide dropped in at the moment it went up.

It's the difference between a 70-minute video and something you can read in ten.

What you get first is a strip of slides you tidy up by clicking. Keep one, drop one, crop one, or grab the picture that's on screen right now if a good one got missed. Then save the whole thing as a document: the words with the slides in them, exactly as spoken. If you'd rather have it tidied into something more readable, that's a different button.

Every slide lands at the second it appeared. Ask for the tidied-up version and the writing gets smoothed out around them — a slide can shift to where it makes more sense to read, but none of them go missing.

This is the part nothing else does for you: it needs the video and every word said in it, side by side. I haven't found anything else that has both.

  • No duplicates

    A slide left up for a minute gives you one picture, not sixty. The near-identical ones are gone before you ever see them.

  • Only the ones with something on them

    Sift reads each picture, and the ones with no words or numbers on them get dropped.

  • Or just the pictures

    “Save slides…” saves the slides you kept as full-size pictures, in case you want them outside the document.

Being honest about the messy part: a recording that keeps cutting back to the speaker and the audience gives you some pictures of a room. There's a setting that throws out the wide shots automatically. Beyond that, it's a few seconds of clicking to drop the ones you don't want.

The slide pictures live in Sift's own folder rather than sitting next to your video — there's a button on every video that opens the folder if you want them.

The words

Even when there are no captions.

If the video already has captions, Sift just takes them. That's instant, and it's as good as whoever wrote them — a proper caption track is better than anything a machine will give you, and an automatic one comes with the mistakes you'd expect.

If there are no captions at all, Sift listens to the video and writes down what's said. That part is AI, and it runs on your own computer rather than on somebody's service, so nothing is sent anywhere for it. Either way you end up with a transcript — every word, with the time it was said — which is what makes the video clickable and the slide document possible.

It isn't English-only. Other languages work, and you can tell Sift which ones you care about instead of leaving it to guess.

Two honest things about that. It isn't instant — your computer is doing the listening, so a one-hour talk with no captions takes ten or fifteen minutes on a fast desktop. On a laptop I'd expect roughly half an hour to forty-five minutes, though I haven't timed one. You can start a batch and go and do something else.

The first time it has to listen to something, there's a one-off half-gigabyte download. After that it works with no internet at all, and the audio never goes anywhere.

  • Files you already have

    Drag a video or an audio file onto the window and it skips straight to being written down. Your file isn't copied or moved — Sift reads it where you keep it, and removing it from the library leaves the original where it was.

  • Subtitles, if you want them

    A transcript can be saved as a subtitle file, one click. Drop it next to the video and it plays with the words on screen, in whatever you watch things in. Handy for sending a talk to somebody who'd rather read along than listen.

Summaries

The summaries need your own AI account.

This is the one thing Sift can't just do for you. It doesn't come with AI and it doesn't sell you any. You connect an account of your own, use one you already pay for, or run it free on your own machine.

  • An account of your own — you set up an account with one of the AI companies. It's the pay-as-you-go kind, where you put five or ten dollars in and it comes off as you use it. Setting one up takes about five minutes, and a summary of a normal-length video costs a few cents. You pay them directly; none of it goes through me.
  • Or the Claude you already use — if you've got Claude's command-line tool installed and signed in, Sift can hand the work to that instead. Nothing extra to pay.
  • Or nothing at all — there's free AI you can run on your own computer instead. It's a separate free program you install once: Sift has a button that takes you straight to it, and tells you the moment it's connected. It's slower than the paid kind, and it wants a computer with a bit of muscle — if yours is more than five or six years old, expect to wait.

A long video costs more than a short one, and the smartest AI costs more than the cheapest. That's the whole of it — it's between you and them, and I don't take a cut.

If you connect a paid account, your account details are locked away on your computer. Sift only ever uses them to talk to the one company you picked, and never sends them anywhere else.

Ask for whatever you actually want.

A summary is only one of the things you can ask for. Sift comes with a handful of ready-made requests — chapter markers, title ideas, a description, show notes, a blog post, a newsletter, the bits worth clipping. A request is just an instruction written in plain English, like “pull out the five best quotes.” You can read every one of them, change the wording, or write your own.

  • Chapters that point at real moments

    Ask for chapters and the times come from what was actually said, not from a guess. Paste them straight under a video and they land where they should.

  • Try a request before you keep it

    Settings has a scratch pad where you paste in some text and try your wording on it, so you can get the request right without making a mess of your library. Nothing you try there is saved.

  • Send your requests to someone

    Save the whole set of requests to a file and email it to a friend, or load a set somebody sends you. If one of theirs is named the same as one of yours, it replaces yours — so rename anything you want to keep first.

In bulk

Paste a stack of links and walk away.

Sift works through them one at a time — saving, writing down, summarising — and keeps going without you. Pause it, shuffle what's waiting, retry only the ones that went wrong. Close the app halfway through and it carries on where it stopped.

Video sites change how they work every few weeks, and that's what stops most downloaders working. Sift quietly keeps itself current so it carries on working. The app itself never updates without asking you first, and there's a setting if you'd rather approve everything.

Your library

One video is a document.
A hundred is a library.

Everything you've saved lands in one place. The whole point of that place is finding it again six months later.

  • Search everything anyone said

    Not just titles. Type a phrase you half-remember hearing and the video comes back, along with the line it came from.

  • Show me these — or hide these

    Click two labels and you'll only see the videos that have both. Right-click a label instead and everything with it disappears — handy when you want everything except the cooking videos.

  • Narrow it down by who made it

    Or by where it came from. The filters sit next to the search box and apply to everything, not just the page you're looking at.

  • Click a line, jump to that moment

    The words scroll along with the video while it plays, and any line you click takes you straight there.

  • Two ways to look at it

    Thumbnails when you want to see what things are, or a sortable list when you want to see who made it, how long it is and when you saved it, all lined up in columns.

  • Watch it somewhere else

    Make a playlist out of whatever you're currently looking at and open it in the media player or TV app you already use.

Channels

Track a channel, not just a link.

Follow a channel and Sift keeps count of what's new since you last looked. Browse its videos, shorts and live streams, tick the ones you want, and send them all off at once. And if you sign in to YouTube inside Sift, it can pull in the channels you already subscribe to, so you don't type them in one at a time.

  • It flags the ones that took off

    A video that did much better than that channel's usual gets a small badge showing how much better. It's a rough signal, not a measurement — treat it as “worth a look”.

  • Signing in

    Members-only and age-checked videos need you to be logged in. Sift opens a browser window of its own, you sign in the normal way, and that stays on your computer. If it goes stale later Sift says so, rather than quietly failing.

Your files

Ordinary files, in a folder you picked.

Videos, transcripts and summaries are saved into a Sift folder inside your Downloads, and you can move that anywhere you like. They're ordinary files — the video plays in whatever you normally use, and the transcript and the summary open in Notepad, Word, or anything else, whether Sift is running or not. Every video has a list of the files Sift created for it, one click to open each.

What never leaves your computer

  • Your videos. The ones you saved and the ones you had.
  • Every transcript, and every summary.
  • Your AI account details, locked away where only Sift can read them.
  • Anything you signed into. That goes to the site you signed into and nowhere else.
  • Everything you searched, labelled or filtered. Every video you looked at and didn't save.

One exception, and it's the obvious one: when you ask for a summary, the words get sent to whichever AI service you picked. Pick the free one that runs on your own computer and even that stops.

What Sift connects to

  • The site you're saving the video from.
  • A check for a new version when it starts, plus the quiet background fixes that keep Sift working with video sites.
  • Thumbnails, fetched once and then kept.
  • The AI account you chose, if you chose one.
  • The one-off download that lets Sift listen to a video, the first time you need it.

None of it is reported back to me — there's no account and no analytics here. The site you're saving from sees the request, the way it would in a browser, and the AI service you picked sees the words you deliberately send it.

Price

It's free. There's no catch and no plan to add one.

No subscription, no account, no trial, no upsell. The app collects nothing about you — no analytics, no tracking, no crash reports. There's nothing to sign up for and nothing to hand over. I'm not building a business on this.

  • $0 for Sift, permanently. Free to use, and the code is public if you want to check it.
  • A few cents for a summary, if you use a paid AI account. Paid to them, not to me.
  • $0 for a summary, if you run the free AI on your own computer — or use a Claude subscription you already pay for.

The obvious objection

One person, no money coming in — so what happens in eighteen months? Fair question. Two things make it survivable. Sift keeps up with the video sites on its own, so saving videos keeps working whether or not I put out another release. And everything Sift makes is an ordinary file in a folder you chose — so if I stopped tomorrow, you'd lose new features and nothing else. The code is public and anyone can carry it on.

Fine print

Six things to know first.

  1. Windows today.

    That's the only version there is right now. Linux is coming. Mac only if enough people ask for one. So if you're reading this on a Mac there's nothing to install today — the rest of the page still tells you what it does, and there's a place at the bottom to say you want one.

  2. Windows will warn you the first time.

    It'll say it doesn't recognise the publisher. That's because I haven't paid to have it signed, which costs money I'm not spending on a free app. Click More info, then Run anyway. After that Sift offers you its own updates — it asks every time and never installs one behind your back — so you shouldn't see that warning again.

  3. You don't have to take my word for it.

    The code is public. Anyone who wants to can read exactly what's in it, including someone you trust more than me.

  4. One big download, once.

    The first time you ask for the words on a video that has no captions, Sift downloads about half a gigabyte, one time. That's what lets it listen to a video and write down what's said without sending the audio anywhere. It never downloads it again, and after that the listening works with no internet at all.

  5. It's new.

    Sift is about a month old. It does what this page says today, but it hasn't been hammered on by thousands of people yet. Everything it makes is an ordinary file in a folder you chose, which is the main reason that matters less than it might.

  6. What you save is your business.

    Sift does what you point it at and takes no view on what you should point it at. What's allowed varies by site and by what you do with the result, and that part is on you.

Questions

Questions people actually ask.

Is it really free?

Yes. No account, no trial, no paid version waiting behind it. The only thing you might pay for is an AI account for the summaries, and that money goes to them, not to me — and there's a free option that runs on your own computer instead.

How long does it take?

Saving a video takes about as long as your connection needs. If the video already has captions, the words are there in seconds. If it doesn't, your computer has to listen to the whole thing — ten or fifteen minutes for an hour of talking on a fast desktop, and I'd expect roughly half an hour to forty-five minutes on a laptop, though I haven't timed one. You can line up a batch and go and do something else.

Where do my files go?

Into a Sift folder inside your Downloads, and you can move that anywhere. The video, the transcript and the summary are ordinary files that open in whatever you already use. The slide pictures are the exception — they live in Sift's own folder rather than sitting next to the video, and there's a button on every video that opens that folder.

Do I have to give you anything?

No. There's no account and no sign-up, and nothing in the app reports anything about you back to me. It checks my releases page for a new version, and that's the whole of it. Your videos, your transcripts and your sign-ins stay on your computer. The one thing that ever leaves is the text of a video you deliberately send off for a summary, and that goes to whichever AI service you picked. This website has no form on it at all.

Can I do a whole channel at once?

Yes. Follow a channel and you can browse everything on it, tick the ones you want, and send the lot off in one go. A playlist link on its own only saves the one video you pasted.

Mac? Linux?

Linux is coming. Mac only if enough people want one — it's real work and there's no point doing it for nobody. If you want one, say so on the project page — it's a free sign-up and it takes a minute — or just email me. That's how I'll count, and it's the only counting there is.

What happens when a site changes and breaks it?

YouTube and the rest change how they work every few weeks, and that's what makes most download tools quietly stop working. Sift fixes itself for those changes on its own, without waiting for a whole new version of the app, and you don't have to install anything for it. If you'd rather approve each fix yourself, there's a setting.

Is it legal to save this stuff?

Sift runs on your computer and does what you tell it to. It doesn't decide what's alright to save — that's on you, and this isn't legal advice. What's allowed varies by site and by what you plan to do with the result.
